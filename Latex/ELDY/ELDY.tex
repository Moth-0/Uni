\chapter{Electrostatics}
\section{Electric field}
The force on a test charge $(Q)$ from a single point charge $(q)$, a distance $\pmb{\r}$ away, is calculated by Coulomb's law: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{F} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \ep}\frac{q Q}{\r^2}\hat{\pmb{\r}}, \eq{2.1}
\end{align*}
where  $\ep = 8.85\ti{-12}\frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{N}\cdot \text{m}^2}$ is the permittivity of free space. 

If we have several point charges, at different distances from Q, we have an elektric field: 
\begin{align*}
    &\v{F} = Q\v{E}, \eq{2.3} \\
    &\v{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\ep}\sum_i \frac{q_i}{\r_i^2}\hat{\pmb{\r}_i}. \eq{2.4}
\end{align*}

If we have a continuous charge distribution, we can calculate the electric field by:
\begin{align*}
    \v{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\ep}\int \frac{1}{\r^2}\hat{\pmb{\r}}dq, \eq{2.5}
\end{align*}
where $dq$ is the charge in a small element, be it line charge $(dq=λdl)$, area charge $(dq=σda)$  or volume charge $(dq=ρdτ)$.

\subsubsection{Special cases:} 
$ $

Field of infinite length wire: \[ E = \frac{1}{4π\ep}\frac{2λ}{z}\]

Field of infinite plane: \[ \v{E} = \frac{σ}{2\ep}\hat{\v{a}}\]

Field outside a uniformly charged sphere with total charge $q$, $r>R$: \[ \v{E} = \frac{1}{4π\ep}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat{\v{r}}\]

\section{Divergence and Curl}
Field lines are imaginary lines used to represent the direction and strength of a field, such as an electric field. They start on positive charges and end on negative charges. The density of these lines indicates the field's strength; closer lines mean a stronger field.

Flux refers to the quantity of a field passing through a given surface. For an electric field, it is the product of the electric field strength and the area through which the field lines pass, considering the angle between the field lines and the surface.
\begin{align*}
    \Phi = \int_{\mathcal{S}} \v{E} \cdot d\v{a} \eq{2.11}
\end{align*}

Gauss's law states that the total electric flux through a closed surface is proportional to the charge enclosed within that surface.
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{E} \cdot d\v{a} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\ep}, \eq{2.13}
\end{align*}
where $Q_{\text{enc}}$ is the charge enclosed by the surface. 
It can be rewritten with the divergence thereom, when $Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_{\mathcal{V}}ρdτ$: 
\begin{align*}
    \nabla \cdot \v{E} = \frac{ρ}{\ep}. \eq{2.14}
\end{align*} 

To calculate the curl of the electric fgield we see that: 
\begin{align*}
    \int_{\v{a}}^{\v{b}} \v{E} \cdot d\v{l} = \frac{1}{4π\ep} \br{\frac{q}{r_a}-\frac{q}{r_b}}, \eq{2.18}
\end{align*}
which on a closed interval gives us: 
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{E} \cdot d\v{l} = 0, \eq{2.19} \\
    \nabla \times \v{E} = \v{0}. \eq{2.20}
\end{align*}

\section{Work and Energy}
If you want to move a test charge $(Q)$ from point $\v{a}$ to point $\v{b}$, 
you have to do work against the electric field. 
The work done is the potential difference between the two points: 
\begin{align*}
    W = Q\br[2]{V(\v{b}) - V(\v{a})}. \eq{2.38}
\end{align*}
So if you want to move the point from ininfity to a point $\v{r}$, and you have the reference point in infinity: 
\begin{align*}
    W = QV(\v{r}). \eq{2.39}
\end{align*}

The work to move a collection of charges is:
\begin{align*}
    W &= \frac{1}{4π\ep}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j>i}^{n}\frac{q_iq_j}{\r_{ij}}, \eq{2.40}\\
    &= \frac{1}{8π\ep}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\frac{q_iq_j}{\r_{ij}}, \eq{2.41} \\
    &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_iV(\v{r}_i). \eq{2.42}
\end{align*}

For continuous charge distributions:
\begin{align*}
    W = \frac{1}{2}\int V dq, \eq{2.43}
\end{align*}
where again, $dq$ is the charge in a small element, be it line charge $(dq=λdl)$, area charge $(dq=σda)$  or volume charge $(dq=ρdτ)$.

If we take (2.43) for volume over all space, we get: 
\begin{align*}
    W = \frac{\ep}{2}\int E^2 dτ. \eq{2.45}
\end{align*}

Energy of Q spread uniformly throughout sphere of radius R: 
\begin{align*}
    W_{tot} &= W_{out}+W_{in} \\
    &= \frac{Q^2}{8π\ep R} + \frac{3Q^2}{20π\ep R}
\end{align*}

\section{Conductors}
A conducter is a material where the electrons are free to move around. The electric field inside a conductor is zero, and the charges are distributed on the surface, like metal.
The electric field inside a conductor is zero, and when it is placed in an electric field,

Outside the conductor, the electric field is the same as if the conductor was not there.

\section{Laplace's equation}
To easily calculate the potential in a region, we can use Laplace's equation:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla^2 V = 0. \eq{3.4}
\end{align*}

In one dimension the solution is:
\begin{align*}
    V(x) = ax + b, \eq{3.6}
\end{align*}

but for 2 dimensions it is not easy to calculate exactly, but you can caluculate $V(x,y)$ as the average of the potential around the point: 
\begin{align*}
    V(x,y) = \frac{1}{2πR}\oint_{circle}Vdl, 
\end{align*}
which might make no sence, but it can be used to calculate the charge distribution, when boundary conditions are known. 

In three dimensions the value of $V$ at point $\v{r}$ is the average value of $V$ over a spherical surface of radius $R$ centered at $\v{r}$: 
\begin{align*}
    V(\v{r}) = \frac{1}{4πR^2}\oint_{\text{sphere}}Vda. 
\end{align*}

\textbf{First uniqueness theorem:} The solution to Laplace’s equation in 
some volume $\mathcal{V}$ is uniquely determined if $V$ is specified on the 
boundary surface $\mathcal{S}$. Meaning that it doesn't matter how you get your solution, 
if it satisfies Laplace’s equation, and the boundary values are correct, then the answer is correct. 

\textbf{Second uniqueness theorem:} In a volume $\mathcal{V}$ surrounded by 
conductors and containing a specified charge density $\rho$, the electric field 
is uniquely determined if the \textit{total charge} on each conductor is given. 
(The region as a whole can be bounded by another conductor, 
or else unbounded.)


\section{The Method of Images}
The method of images can be used to solve advanced electrostatic problems, 
by replacing conductors with fictitious charges that mimic the same boundary conditions. 

For example, we have a infinite grounded plate, with a point charge a distance $d$ above it. 
The boundary potential is that $V=0$ when $z=0$ and that $V \rightarrow 0$ far from the charge (Fig 3.10).
The same boundary conditions are satisfied if we have a point charge at $-d$ instead of the conductor (Fig 3.11). 
Therefore the potential for a pointcharge above an infinite grounded plate is: 
\begin{align*}
    V(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{4π\ep}\br[2]{\frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2}}-\frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+d)^2}}}. \eq{3.9}
\end{align*}

From this we can calculate the induced surface charge on the conductor: 
\begin{align*}
    σ(x,y) &= -\ep \frac{\dd V}{\dd z}|_{z=0} \\
    &= \frac{-qd}{2π(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{3/2}}, \eq{3.10}
\end{align*}
and the total charge: 
\begin{align*}
    Q = \int σda = -q. \eq{3.11}
\end{align*}

We can also use the method of images to find the force on the point charge due to the induction: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{F} = -\frac{1}{4π\ep} \frac{q^2}{(2d)^2}\hat{\v{z}}, \eq{3.12}
\end{align*}
but the work will not be the same for the plane, and the point charge, 
so if we want to calculate the energy to move the charge from infinity to d as:
\begin{align*}
    W = \int_{\infty}^{d}\v{F}\cdot d\v{l}= -\frac{1}{4π\ep}\frac{q^2}{4d}. \eq{3.14}
\end{align*}

You can not put the image charge in the reagion where you are calculating the potential, 
so in example 3.2, where we have a point charge a distance a away from a sperical grounded conductor, 
the image charge is inside the sphere. 

This gives the potential: 
\begin{align*}
    V(\v{r}) = \frac{1}{4π\ep}\br{\frac{q}{\r}+\frac{q'}{\r'}}, \eq{3.17}
\end{align*}
where $q'$ is the image charge, and $\r$ and $\r'$ is the distance from $q$ and $q'$.

To calculate the potential from a multipole we use:
\begin{align*}
    V(\v{r}) = \frac{1}{4π\ep}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int(r')^nP_n(\cos(α))ρ(\v{r}')dτ', \eq{3.95}
\end{align*}
where $P_l$ is the Legendre polynomial, $ρ$ is the total charge of the distrubution. 
$n = 0 $ for monopole, $1$ for dipole and so on. 

For monopole and dipole we get: 
\begin{align*}
    V_{mon}(\v{r}) = \elcon \frac{Q}{r}, \eq{3.97} \\
    V_{dip}(\v{r}) = \elcon \frac{\v{p}\cdot \hat{\v{r}}}{r^2}, \eq{3.99}
\end{align*}
where $\v{p}$ is the dipole moment of the distrubution: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{p} = \int \v{r}'ρ(\v{r}')dτ'. \eq{3.98}
\end{align*}

The electric field of a dipole is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{E}_{dip}(r,θ) = \frac{p}{4π\ep r^3}\br{2\cos(θ)\hat{\v{r}}+\sin(θ)\hat{\v{θ}}}. \eq{3.103}
\end{align*}

\chapter{Electric Fields in Matter}
\section{Dielectrics}
If you put a neutrol atom in an electric field, it is either ripped apart, being ionized, 
or it reaches an equilibrium, where the force from the electric field and the force holding the atom toghether cancel eachother, 
making the atom polarized. This will cause the atom to have a dipolemoment $\v{p}$, with the same direction as $\v{E}$. 
\begin{align*}
    \v{p} = α\v{E} \eq{4.1}, 
\end{align*}
here $α$ is called the atomic polarizability, and is dependent on the atoms structure. 

If an atom was made by a center charge $q$ surrounded by as spherical charged cloud $-q$ with radius $a$, 
then the electric field we place the atom in, would move the nucleus a distance $d$ from the center. 
Now the electric field in equilibrium would be: 
\begin{align*}
    E &= \elcon \frac{qd}{a^3}, \quad \text{or} \quad p = qd = (4π\ep a^3)E, \\
    \Rightarrow α &= 4π\ep a^3 = 3\ep v, \eq{4.2}
\end{align*}
where $v$ is the volume of the atom. 
\bil{fig4.1}

For molecules it is not so easy, as the dipolemoment might not be the same direction as E, 
and therefore we get 9 polarizability constants: 
\begin{align*}
    \begin{cases}
        p_x = \alpha_{xx} E_x + \alpha_{xy} E_y + \alpha_{xz} E_z, \\
        p_y = \alpha_{yx} E_x + \alpha_{yy} E_y + \alpha_{yz} E_z, \\
        p_z = \alpha_{zx} E_x + \alpha_{zy} E_y + \alpha_{zz} E_z.
    \end{cases} \eq{4.3}
\end{align*}


\subsection{Alignment of polar molecules}
Some molecules have their own dipolemoment, such as water, which are called polar molecules. 
If placed in an uniform electric field, the force on the postive end, cancels out with the force on the negative end, 
but there will be a torque 
\begin{align*}
    \v{N} = (\v{r}_+ \times \v{F}_+)+(\v{r}_- \times \v{F}_-) = \v{p}\times \v{E}. \eq{4.4}
\end{align*}
\bil{fig4.4}
$\v{N}$ is in such a direction to line $\v{p}$ parallel to $\v{E}$. 

If the field is not uniform over det atom, which is rare, there is a net force on the atom 
\begin{align*}
    \v{F} = \br{\v{p}\cdot \nabla}\v{E}. \eq{4.5}
\end{align*}

An ideal dipole is the atrifical limit $d\rightarrow 0$ and $q \rightarrow \infty$. 
The energy of an ideal dipolemoment is 
\begin{align*}
    U = -\v{p}\cdot \v{E}, \eq{4.6} 
\end{align*}
and the interaction energy between two ideal dipoles with displacement $\v{r}$ is 
\begin{align*}
    U = \elcon \frac{1}{r^3}\br[2]{\v{p}_1\cdot\v{p}_2-3\br{\v{p}_1\cdot \hat{\v{r}}}\br{\v{p}_2\cdot \hat{\v{r}}}}. \eq{4.7}
\end{align*}

If we place a material made of nonpolar molecules in an electric field,  
it will induce a dipole moment in all the molecules. If we place a polar material, 
the field will turn all the molecules. We get the polarization which is 
\begin{align*}
    \v{P} = \text{dipole moment per unit volume. } 
\end{align*}

\section{Field of a Polarized Object}
To calculate the field that arises from the induced dipoles in a material placed in an electric field, 
we first calculate the potential by integrating each dipole over det volume $\v{p}=\v{P}dτ'$: 
\begin{align*}
    V(\v{r}) &= \elcon \int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{\v{P}(\v{r}')\cdot \hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2}dτ', \eq{4.9} \\
    &= \elcon \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \frac{σ_b}{\r}da'+\elcon \int_{\mathcal{V}}\frac{ρ_b}{\r}dτ', \eq{4.13}
\end{align*}
where we have the surface charge density $σ_b = \v{P}\cdot\hat{\v{n}}$ and the volume charge density $ρ_b = -\v{\nabla}\cdot \v{P}$. 

For a uniformly polarized sphere of radius R, the volume charge density is zero bacause $\v{P}$ is uniform, but 
\begin{align*}
    σ_b = \v{P}\cdot \hat{\v{n}} = P\cos θ,
\end{align*}
from this we get
\begin{align*}
    V(r,θ) = \begin{cases}
        \frac{P}{3\ep}r\cos θ & \text{for } r \leq R, \\
        \frac{P}{3\ep}\frac{R^3}{r^2}\cos θ & \text{for } r \geq R.
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}

So the field inside the sphere is uniform, and outside the sphere it behaves like a ideal dipole, 
whose dipole moment is the total dipole moment of a sphere $\v{p} = \frac{4}{3}πR^3\v{P}$.


\section{Electric Displacement}
Along with the bound charge, there is also fee charge which could be electrons on a fonductor, 
or ions embedded in the dielectric material, so any charge not assosiated with $\v{P}$. 
Within the dielectric, the total charge density is 
\begin{align*}
    ρ = ρ_b + ρ_f, \eq{4.20}
\end{align*}
and Gauss's law is 
\begin{align*}
    \ep \v{\nabla}\cdot\v{E} = ρ = ρ_b + ρ_f = -\v{\nabla}\cdot\v{P}+ρ_f, 
\end{align*}
where $\v{E}$ is the toral field. 

This lead to a term for $ρ_f$: 
\begin{align*}
    ρ_f &= \v{\nabla}\cdot\br{\ep\v{E}+\v{P}}, \\
    &= \v{\nabla}\cdot\v{D}, \eq{4.22} \\
    \Rightarrow \oint &\v{D}\cdot d\v{a} = Q_{f_{enc}}. \eq{4.23}
\end{align*}
Here $\v{D}$ is known as the electric displacement. 

Typically we know $ρ_f$, and we can therefore calculate D. When computing the displacement look for symmetry, 
if there is spherical, cylindircal or plane symmetri we can use (\ref{eq:4.23}) because $\v{\nabla}\times\v{D} = 0$. 

\section{Linear Dielectrics}
The polarization is proportional to the electric field: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{P} = \ep Χ_e \v{E} \eq{4.30}, 
\end{align*}
where $χ_e$ is the electric susceptibility, which is dependant on the structure of the material. 
Materials that obey eq. 4.30 are called linear dielectrics. 
Notice that $\v{E}$ is the total electric field, and therefore we can not calculate $\v{P}$ from eq. 4.30, 
instead we could calculate 
\begin{align*}
    \v{D} = \ep \v{E}+\v{P} = \ep(1+χ_e)\v{E}=\epsilon \v{E}, \eq{4.31}
\end{align*}
where we have the permittivity of the material, and dielectric constant (Table \hyperref[fig:fig4.2]{4.2}):
\begin{align*}
    \epsilon = \ep(1+χ_e), \eq{4.33} \\
    \epsilon_r = 1+χ_e = \frac{\epsilon}{\ep}. \eq{4.34}
\end{align*}

When we have a charge distrubution in homogeneous linear dielectric, the field is calculated as in vacum, but using $\epsilon$ instead of $\ep$. 
To calculate the full energy stored in a electrostatic system in a linear dielectric, we rewrite (\ref{eq:2.45}) to
\begin{align*}
    W = \frac{\ep}{2}\int \epsilon_r E^2dτ = \frac{1}{2}\int \v{D}\cdot \v{E}dτ. \eq{4.58}
\end{align*}

Say we have a dielectric in a capacitor, the force required to remove the dielectric can be calculated as 
\begin{align*}
    F &= -\frac{dW}{dx} = \frac{1}{2} V^2 \frac{dC}{dx}, \eq{4.64} \\
    &= -\frac{\ep χ_e w}{2d}V^2, \eq{4.65}
\end{align*}
where $d$ is the distance between the plates, and $w$ is the width of the plate, perpendicular to the force.

\chapter{Magnetostatics}
\section{Magnetic Forces}
If we have a wire with a current running through, it will create a magnetic field, 
the direction of the filed can be found by the right hand rule, if your thumb is in the direction of current, 
you fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field. 

A force will be induced on a moving charge ($Q$) in a magnetic field, moving with velocity ($\v{v}$)
\begin{align*}
    \v{F}_{mag} = Q\br{\v{v}\times \v{B}}\eq{5.1}. 
\end{align*}
This is the Lorentz force law. When there is both a magnetic field and an electric field, the force would be 
\begin{align*}
    \v{F} = Q\br{\v{E}+\br{\v{v}\times\v{B}}}.\eq{5.2} 
\end{align*}

For a charged particle in a magnetic field, the magnetic force will create a centripetal acceleration, 
which leads to the cycletron formula, used to find the momentum of a charged particle,
\begin{align*}
    QvB = m\frac{v^2}{R}, \quad\text{or } p = QBR. \eq{5.3}
\end{align*}

If we have both a magnetic field and an electric field, we can follow example 5.2, 
to show that the particle will move in a cycloid motion, a circle where the center moves $(0,Rωt,R)$: 
\begin{align*}
    (y-Rωt)^2+&(z-R)^2=R^2 \eq{5.9} \\
    &Rω = \frac{E}{B} \eq{5.10}.
\end{align*}

A magnetic field can not speed up or slow down a particle, it can only change its direction, 
therefore the field does no work on the particle, 
\begin{align*}
    dW_{mag} = \v{F}_{mag}\cdot d\v{l}=Q(\v{v}\times\v{B})\cdot\v{v}dt=0. \eq{5.11}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Current}
A line charge $λ$ traveling down a wire at speed $v$, constitutes a charge
\begin{align*}
    \v{I} = λ\v{v}. \eq{5.14}
\end{align*}

The magnetic force on a segment of current-carrying wire is:
\begin{align*}
    \v{F}_{mag} = \int (\v{I}\times\v{B})dl, \eq{5.15}
\end{align*}
if $\v{I}$ and $d\v{l}$ point in the same direction: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{F}_{mag} = I \int (d\v{l}\times\v{B}). \eq{5.17}
\end{align*}

When charge voves over a surface we describe it by the surface current density
\begin{align*}
    \v{K} = \frac{d\v{I}}{dl_{\perp}} = σ\v{v}. \eq{5.23}
\end{align*}

And the magnetic force on the surface current is
\begin{align*}
    \v{F}_{mag} = \int (\v{K} \times \v{B}) da. \eq{5.24}
\end{align*}

In a volume we have the volume current density: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{J} &= \frac{d\v{I}}{da_{\perp}} = ρ\v{v}, \eq{5.26}\\
    \v{F}_{mag} &= \int (\v{J} \times \v{B}) dτ. \eq{5.27}
\end{align*}

The total charge crossing a surface can be calculated as 
\begin{align*}
    I = \int_{\mathcal{S}}Jda_{\perp} = \int_{\mathcal{S}}\v{J}d\v{a}. \eq{5.28}
\end{align*}

From this we get the continuity equation 
\begin{align*}
    \v{\nabla} \cdot \v{J} = -\frac{\dd ρ}{\dd t}. \eq{5.29}
\end{align*}

\section{Biot-Savart Law}
The magnetic field of a steady line current is given by Biot-Savart law: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{B}(\v{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{\v{I} \times \hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2} dl',
\end{align*}
where $μ_0$ is the permeability of free space. 

The magnitude of the magnetic field caused by an infinite wire is: 
\begin{align*}
    B = \frac{μ_0I}{2πs}, \eq{5.38}
\end{align*}
where the direction follows the right-hand rule. 
For a point a distance $z$ above a circular loop of radius $R$, the field is: 
\begin{align*}
    B(z) = \frac{μ_0I}{2}\frac{R^2}{\br{R^2+z^2}^{3/2}}. \eq{5.41}
\end{align*}

For surface and volume charge the Biot-Savart law becomes: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{B}(\v{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{\v{K} \times \hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2} da', \\
    \v{B}(\v{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{\v{J} \times \hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2} dτ'. \eq{5.42}
\end{align*}

\section{Divergence and Curl of B}
For any wire running through a chosen loop, the integral of $\v{B}$ is: 
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{B} \cdot d\v{l} = μ_0I_{\text{enc}}, \eq{5.44}
\end{align*}
where $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the total current enclosed by the loop. 
If the flow of current is volume current density, the enclosed current is: 
\begin{align*}
    I_{\text{enc}} = \int \v{J}\cdot d\v{a}. \eq{5.45}
\end{align*}
Applying Stokes law, gives us the curl of $\v{B}$ for infinite wires:
\begin{align*}
    \int \br{\nabla \times \v{B}}\cdot d\v{a} = μ_0 \int \v{J} \cdot d\v{a}, \\
    \Rightarrow \nabla \times \v{B} = μ_0 \v{J}. \eq{5.46}
\end{align*}

To find the general curl of $\v{B}$, we use Biot Savart law: 
\begin{align*}
    \nabla \times \v{B} &= \frac{μ_0}{4π} \int \v{J} \times \frac{\hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2} dτ', \eq{5.51} \\
    &= μ_0 \v{J}. \eq{5.56}
\end{align*}

For the divergence of $\v{B}$ we get:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla \cdot \v{B} = 0. \eq{5.50}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Ampère's Law}
Equation (5.56) is called Ampère's law in differential form, if we integrate we get:
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{B}\cdot d\v{l} = μ_0 \int \v{J}\cdot d\v{a} = μ_0 I_{\text{enc}}. \eq{5.57}
\end{align*}
This playes the role of Gauss law for magnetostatics.

\section{Magnetic Vector Potential}
We introduce the vector potential $\v{A}$:

which divergence is 0:

\end{align*}
which in most cases leads to the dipole moment being the dominant term:

\chapter{Magnetic Fields in Matter}
\section{Magnetization}
Magnetic material, are magnetic because the electrons spin around their core. this creates magnetic dipoles. 
which in most magnets are turned in the direction of the magnetic field, the material is placed in. 

If we but a rectangulat loop in a uniform field, the loop experiences a torque: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{N} = aFsinθ\hat{\v{x}}, 
\end{align*}
the magnitude of the force is $F=IbB$, which leads to:
\begin{align*}
    \v{N} = \v{m} \times \v{B}, \eq{6.1}
\end{align*}
where $\v{m}=Iab$ is the magnetic dipole moment of the loop.

\bil{fig6.2}

In a uniform magnetic field the net force on a current loop is $0$, 
but in a nonuniform field it is:
\begin{align*}
    \v{F} = \nabla(\v{m}\cdot \v{B}). \eq{6.3}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Atomic Orbits}
If we say electrons revolve around the nucleus in a circular motion with radius $R$,
it will look like a steady current: 
\begin{align*}
    I &= \frac{e}{T} = \frac{e}{2πR/v}, \\
    \Rightarrow \v{m} &= - \frac{1}{2}evR\hat{\v{z}}. \eq{6.4}
\end{align*}

If we put an atom in a magnetic field, it will be hard to tilt the orbit of the electron, 
however the electron will speed up or slow down, depending on the direction of $\v{B}$. 
Without the field we have: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{1}{4π\ep}\frac{e^2}{R^2} = m_e \frac{v^2}{R}, \eq{6.5}
\end{align*}
but in a field there is a force $-e(\v{v}\times \v{B})$, 
if $\v{B}$ is perpendicular on the plane of orbit, we get: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{1}{4π\ep}\frac{e^2}{R^2} + e\bar{v}B= m_e \frac{\bar{v}^2}{R}, \eq{6.6}
\end{align*}
where $\bar{v}$ is the new speed of the electron. We can calculate the difference assuming the change in speed is small:
\begin{align*}
    Δv = \bar{v}-v = \frac{eRB}{2m_e}. \eq{6.7}
\end{align*}

This also leads to a change in dipole moment, opposide the fields direction: 
\begin{align*}
    Δ\v{m} = -\frac{1}{2}eRΔv\hat{\v{z}} = -\frac{e^2R^2}{4m_e}\v{B}. \eq{6.8}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Magnetization}
So far we have seen two kinds of Magnetization. 
\textit{Paramagnetism} where the dipoles associated with spins of unpaired electrons experience a torque tending to line them up parallel to the field, and 
\textit{Diamagnetism} where the speed of the electrons is altered in such a way to change the obital dipole moment in a direction opposide the field. 
These effect can be decribed by the \textit{magnetization} $\v{M}$, which is the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. 

\section{Field of a Magnetized Object}
With the magnetization we can calculate the vector potentiel from (\ref{eq:5.85}) 
knowing that $\v{m} = \v{M}dτ'$:
\begin{align*}
    \v{A}(\v{r}) = \frac{μ_0}{4π} \int \frac{\v{M}(\v{r}') \times \hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2}dτ', \eq{6.11}
\end{align*}
which can also be rewritten in terms of the potential of a volume current $\v{J}_b = \nabla \times \v{M}$ and surface current $\v{K}_b = \v{M} \times \hat{\v{n}}$:
\begin{align*}
    \v{A}(\v{r}) = \frac{μ_0}{4π} \int_{\mathscr{V}} \frac{\v{J}_b(\v{r}')}{\r}dτ' + \frac{μ_0}{4π} \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \frac{\v{K}_b(\v{r}')}{\r}da'. \eq{6.15}
\end{align*}

\section{Auxiliary Field}
We can now collect the field due to bound charge, and the field due to the free charge. 
\begin{align*}
    \v{J} = \v{J}_b + \v{J}_f. \eq{6.17}
\end{align*}
With Amperes law we get: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{1}{μ_0} (\nabla \times \v{B}) &= \v{J} = \v{J}_b + \v{J}_f = \v{J}_f + (\nabla \times \v{M}), \\ 
    &\Rightarrow \nabla \times \br{\frac{1}{μ_0}\v{B} - \v{M}} = \v{J}_f, 
\end{align*}
We define $\v{H}$: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{H} = \frac{1}{μ_0}\v{B} - \v{M}, \eq{6.18} \\
    \Rightarrow \nabla \times \v{H} = \v{J}_f, \eq{6.19}
\end{align*}
and in integral form: 
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{H} \cdot d\v{l} = I_{\text{fenc}}, \eq{6.20}
\end{align*}
where $I_{\text{fenc}}$ is the free current through the loop. 


\section{Linear and Nonlinear Media}
In paramagnetic and diamagnetic materials, the magnetization is sustained by the field. 
Which means $\v{M}$ is proportional to $\v{B}$ and therefore also $\v{H}$: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{M} = χ_m \v{H}, \eq{6.29}
\end{align*}
where $χ_m$ is the magnetic susceptibility, and is dependent of material. 
Materials that obey (\ref{eq:6.29}) are called linear media. 
This gives us: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{B} = μ\v{H}, \eq{6.31}\\
    \Rightarrow μ=μ_0(1+χ_m), \eq{6.32}
\end{align*}
which is the permeability of the material (Table \hyperref[fig:tab6.1]{6.1}). 

\subsection{Ferromagnatism}
In ferromagnetic materials, the alignement of sipoles is "frozen", 
because each dipole like to face the same way as their neighbours. 
We split the material up into domains, which can have different alignmenets, 
which explains why not all iron tools are magnetic. 
When a magnetic field is applied, the domains will compete and move their bounderies, 
so the domains with dipoles facing parallel to the field will grow. 

\chapter{Electrodynamics}
\section{Electromotive Force}
To make current flow, you have to "push" the charges. 
For most materials the current density $\v{J}$ is proportional to the force per unit charge $\v{f}$:
\begin{align*}
    \v{J} = σ\v{f}, \eq{7.1}
\end{align*}
where $σ$ is the conductivity of the material, which is sometimes written as resistivity $ρ = 1/σ$ (Table \hyperref[fig:tab7.1]{7.1}). 
Usually it is a electromagnetic forces that "pushes" and (\ref{eq:7.1}) becomes:
\begin{align*}
    \v{J} = σ(\v{E} + \v{v} \times \v{B}), \eq{7.2}	
\end{align*}
but the velocity of the charges is small enough that we can ignore the term, 
and we get Ohm's Law: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{J} = σ\v{E}, \eq{7.3}
\end{align*}
which might not seem famillar, but if we use (\ref{eq:7.3}) to find the potential difference between two electrodes, 
we get: 
\begin{align*}
    V=IR. \eq{7.4}
\end{align*}

As electrons travels down a wire, they collide with the atoms in the material, and eachother. 
This results in energy given off as heat in the resistor: 
\begin{align*}
    P = VI = I^2R. \eq{7.7}
\end{align*}

The reason a light turns on instantly when you close a switch, 
is because if the if the charges built up at a point in a circuit, it will create an electric field, 
that will then restore uniform flow. This electric field propegates through the circuit at lightspeed, 
hence why the light turns on almost instantly. 

So there are two forces that drive the current, the electric field and the source, often chemical force from a battery: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{f} = \v{f}_s + \v{E}. \eq{7.8}
\end{align*}
The electromotive force or \textit{emf}, is determined by: 
\begin{align*}
    \mathscr{E} = \oint \v{f} \cdot d\v{l} = \oint \v{f}_s \cdot d\v{l}. \eq{7.9}
\end{align*}
In an ideal emf we have $\v{E} = - \v{f}_s$, and therefore: 
\begin{align*}
    V = \int_{a}^{b} \v{f}_s\cdot d\v{l} = \mathscr{E}. \eq{7.10}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Motional emf}
Suppose you move part of a current loop through a uniform magnetic field, 
with Lorentz law this will induce a current in the loop. 
\begin{align*}
    \mathscr{E} = \oint \v{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\v{l}=vBh, \eq{7.11}
\end{align*}
where the loop is rectangular with height h. But the magnetic force does not do work on the loop, 
so the person moving the loop is doing the work. 
The force required is $f_{\text{pull}} = uB$ where $u$ is the vertical speed of the charges. 
This leads to the work done by unit charge: 
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{f}_{\text{pull}}\cdot d\v{l} = vBh = \mathscr{E}. 
\end{align*}
The flux of $\v{B}$ through a loop is: 
\begin{align*}
    Φ = \int \v{B}\cdot d\v{a}. \eq{7.12}
\end{align*}

Instead of calculating for each charge, we use the flux through the loop:
\begin{align*}
    Φ = & xBh, \quad
    \frac{dΦ}{dt} = -vBh, \\
    &\Rightarrow \mathscr{E} = -\frac{dΦ}{dt}. \eq{7.13}
\end{align*}

\section{Electromagnetic Induction}
Faraday found that if he moved the magnetic field instead of the loop, current would still run through the loop.
But it can't be the magnetic field moving the charges, instead it is an induced electric field. 
\begin{align*}
    \mathscr{E} = \oint \v{E}\cdot d\v{l} = -\frac{dΦ}{dt}, \eq{7.23}
\end{align*}
this leads to Faraday's law: 
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{E}\cdot d\v{l} &= -\int \frac{\dd\v{B}}{\dd t} \cdot d\v{a} \eq{7.24} \\
    \nabla \times \v{E} &= -\frac{\dd\v{B}}{\dd t}. \eq{7.25}
\end{align*}
Toghether with this Lenz's law say that the induced current is in such a direction that it opposes the change in magnetic flux.

If $\v{E}$ is a pure Faraday field, due only to the change in $\v{B}$, then the calculations are analogoue to magnetostatics:
\begin{align*}
    \v{E} = -\frac{1}{4π}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int \frac{\v{B} \times \hat{\pmb{\r}}}{\r^2}dτ, \eq{7.27}
\end{align*}
and if we have symmetry we can use Amperes law: 
\begin{align*}
    \oint \v{E}\cdot d\v{l} = -\frac{dΦ}{dt}. \eq{7.28}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Inductance}
If we have two loops of wire, and a current runs in one of the wires, this creates a magnetic field.
This magnetic field will have a flux through the other loop, which is proportional to the current: 
\begin{align*}
    Φ = MI, \eq{7.31}
\end{align*}
where $M$ is the mutual inductance. $M$ is a geometrical property, and is the same for if the current was running in loop 2. 
\begin{align*}
    M_{12} = M_{21} = \frac{μ_0}{4π}\oint \oint \frac{d\v{l}_2\cdot d\v{l}_2}{\r}. \eq{7.32}
\end{align*}
If the current is not steady we see that an emf is induced in the other loop: 
\begin{align*}
    \mathscr{E}_2 = -\frac{dΦ_2}{dt} = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt}. \eq{7.34}
\end{align*}

On top of that it also induces an emf in itself: 
\begin{align*}
    Φ = LI, \eq{7.35}
\end{align*}
where $L$ is the self-inductance, or just inductance. If the current is changing we get: 
\begin{align*}
    \mathscr{E} = -L\frac{dI}{dt}. \eq{7.36}
\end{align*}

When you close a circuit with an inductance $L$ and resistance $R$, the current will build up,
since 
\begin{align*}
    &\emf_0 - L\frac{dI}{dt} = IR, \\
    \Rightarrow &I(t) = \frac{\emf_0}{R}\br{1-\e{-(R/L)t}}, \eq{7.38}
\end{align*}
if $I(0) = 0$. 

It takes energy to build up this current in the loop, which can be seen as energy stored in the magnetic field. 
If we start at $I(0)=0$and work up to $I$, we get:
\begin{align*}
    W &= \frac{1}{2}LI^2, \eq{7.39} \\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \oint (\v{A}\cdot\v{I})dl, \eq{7.40}\\
    &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathcal{V}}(\v{A}\cdot\v{J})dτ, \eq{7.41} \\
    &= \frac{1}{2μ_0} \int_{\text{all space}} B^2dτ. \eq{7.44}
\end{align*}

\section{Maxwell's Equations}
So far we have 4 equations: 
\begin{align*}
    (i)\quad &\v{\nabla} \cdot \v{E} = \frac{ρ}{\ep} & (\text{Gauss's law}), \\
    (ii)\quad &\v{\nabla} \cdot \v{B} = 0 & (\text{No name}), \\
    (iii)\quad &\v{\nabla} \times \v{E} = -\frac{\dd \v{B}}{\dd t} & (\text{Faraday's law}), \\
    (iv)\quad &\v{\nabla} \times \v{B} = μ_0\v{J} & (\text{Ampère's law}).
\end{align*}
But there is a problem with Ampère's law, since it is only for magnetostatics. 
Maxwell fixed this problem and the $(iv)$ equation becomes:
\begin{align*}
    \v{\nabla} \times \v{B} = μ_0\v{J} + μ_0ε_0\frac{\dd \v{E}}{\dd t}. \eq{7.46}
\end{align*}

Griffiths write the Maxwell equations like this: 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{rcases}
        (i)\quad \v{\nabla} \cdot \v{E} = \frac{ρ}{\ep}, & (iii)\quad \v{\nabla} \times \v{E} + \frac{\dd \v{B}}{\dd t} = \pmb{0},\\
        (ii)\quad \v{\nabla} \cdot \v{B} = 0, & (iv)\quad \v{\nabla} \times \v{B} - μ_0\ep\frac{\dd \v{E}}{\dd t} = μ_0\v{J}.
    \end{rcases} \eq{7.52}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Maxwell's Equations in Matter}
If the polarization of a material changes it induces a current: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{J}_p = \frac{\dd \v{P}}{\dd t}, \eq{7.58}
\end{align*}
which means that the total current density is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{J} = \v{J}_f + \v{J}_b + \v{J}_p = \v{J}_f + \nabla \times \v{M}+\frac{\dd\v{P}}{\dd t}. \eq{7.60}
\end{align*}
With this we can write Maxwell's equations for free charge and current: 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{rcases}
        (i)\quad \v{\nabla} \cdot \v{D} = ρ_f, & (iii)\quad \v{\nabla} \times \v{E} = -\frac{\dd \v{B}}{\dd t},\\
        (ii)\quad \v{\nabla} \cdot \v{B} = 0, & (iv)\quad \v{\nabla} \times \v{H} = \v{J}_f+\frac{\dd \v{H}}{\dd t},
    \end{rcases} \eq{7.65}
\end{equation*}
where 
\begin{align*}
    &\v{D} = \varepsilon \v{E} \quad \text{and} \quad \v{H} = \frac{1}{μ} \v{B}, \eq{7.67} \\
    &\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1+χ_e) \quad \text{and} \quad μ = μ_0(1+χ_m). 
\end{align*}

\chapter{Conservation Laws}
\section{Charge and energy}
Because of local charge conservation, charge must flow through a surface to leave a system: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{dQ}{dt} = -\oint_{\mathcal{S}} \v{J}\cdot d\v{a}, \eq{8.2}
\end{align*}
which leads to the continuity equation: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{dρ}{dt} = -\nabla \cdot \v{J}. \eq{8.4}
\end{align*}

From the work required to assamble a static charge distrubution (\ref{eq:2.45}), 
and the work required to get current to move against the back emf (\ref{eq:7.44}), 
we get the total stored energy in electromagnetic fields, per unit volume: 
\begin{align*}
    u = \frac{1}{2} \br{\ep E^2 + \frac{1}{μ_0}B^2}. \eq{8.5}
\end{align*}
The rate of work done on all charges in a volume is: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{dW}{dt} = \int \v{E}\cdot\v{J} dτ, \eq{8.6}
\end{align*}
which can be rewritten using Ampère-Maxwell law: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{dW}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{1}{2} \br{\ep E^2 + \frac{1}{μ_0}B^2} dτ - \frac{1}{μ_0} \oint_{\mathcal{S}} (\v{E} \times \v{B})\cdot d\v{a}. \eq{8.9}
\end{align*}
This is Poynting's theorem. The first integral is the total energy stored in the electromagnetic field, 
the secon integral is the energy leaving the volume $\mathcal{V}$. 
The energy per unit time, per unit area, transported by the fields is called the Poynting vector: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{S} = \frac{1}{μ_0}(\v{E} \times \v{B}). \eq{8.10}
\end{align*}
Now we can rewrite (\ref{eq:8.9}) again: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{dW}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_{\mathcal{V}} u dτ - \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \v{S}\cdot d\v{a}. \eq{8.11}
\end{align*}
This leads to a general continuity equtaion for energy in electromagnetic fields: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\dd u}{\dd t} = -\nabla \cdot \v{S}. \eq{8.12}
\end{align*}

\section{Momentum}
The force per unit volume on charges is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{f} &= ρ\v{E} + \v{J} \times \v{B}, \eq{8.14} \\
    &= \epsilon_0 \left[ (\nabla \cdot \v{E})\v{E} + (\v{E} \cdot \nabla)\v{E} \right]
    + \frac{1}{\mu_0} \left[ (\nabla \cdot \v{B})\v{B} + (\v{B} \cdot \nabla)\v{B} \right] \\
    & \quad - \frac{1}{2} \nabla \left( \epsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0} B^2 \right)
    - \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\v{E} \times \v{B}). \eq{8.16}    
\end{align*}
which can be rewritten using Maxwell stress tensor: 
\begin{align*}
    T_{ij} = \ep \br{E_iE_j-\frac{1}{2}δ_{ij}E^2}+\frac{1}{μ_0} \br{B_iB_j-\frac{1}{2}δ_{ij}B^2}. \eq{8.17}
\end{align*}
This leads to force per unit volume: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{f} = \nabla \cdot \v{T} - \ep μ_0 \frac{\dd S}{\dd t}, \eq{8.19}
\end{align*}
and total force: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{F} = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \v{T} \cdot d\v{a} - \ep μ_0 \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\mathcal{V}} \v{S} dτ. \eq{8.20}
\end{align*}
with this we can rewrite the force per unit volume and total force: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{f} &= \nabla \cdot \v{T} - \ep \frac{\partial \v{S}}{\partial t}, \eq{8.19} \\
    \v{F} &= \frac{d\v{p}}{dt} = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \v{T} \cdot d\v{a} - \ep μ_0 \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\mathcal{V}} \v{S} dτ. \eq{8.20}
\end{align*}

this gives us an expression for the momentum of the charges stored in the field: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{p} = \ep μ_0 \int_{\mathcal{V}} \v{S} dτ, \eq{8.28}
\end{align*}
the momentum density is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{g} = \ep μ_0 \v{S} = \ep (\v{E}\times\v{B}). \eq{8.29}
\end{align*}

There can also be an angular momentum stored in the field, the angular momentum per unit volume is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{l} = \v{r} \times \v{g} = \ep \br[2]{\v{r}\times (\v{E}\times\v{B})}. \eq{8.33}
\end{align*}

\section{Magnetic Forces Do No Work}

According to the Lorentz force law,
\[
\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}),
\]
the magnetic part of the force, $\mathbf{F}_{\text{mag}} = q\, \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}$, is always perpendicular to the velocity $\mathbf{v}$ and therefore does no work:
\[
\mathbf{F}_{\text{mag}} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0.
\]

This implies that magnetic fields cannot increase or decrease the kinetic energy of charged particles.
Despite this, real-life systems (e.g., a magnetic crane lifting a car) seem to contradict this rule. However, Griffiths explains:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The energy comes from external sources (e.g., power supplies), not from the magnetic field itself.
    \item Magnetic fields redirect forces but cannot do work.
    \item Induced electric fields (via Faraday’s law) or motional emf are responsible for actual energy transfer.
    \item Examples (e.g., lifting current loops or magnets) show that the magnetic force is conservative, redistributing energy but not generating it.
\end{itemize}

Any scenario that appears to violate this must be explained through alternative mechanisms (e.g., induced electric fields, energy stored in fields, or mechanical agents).

\chapter{Electromagnetic Waves}
\section{Waves in One Dimension}
Waves propegating in $z$ with a speed $v$, satisfy the wave equation: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\dd^2 f}{\dd z^2} = \frac{1}{v^2} \frac{\dd^2 f}{\dd t^2}, \eq{9.2}
\end{align*}
$v = T/μ$ where $T$ is the tension and $μ$ is the mass per unit length.
The wave equation is linear, so the sum of two solutions is a solution. 
We have 3 different kinds of wave equations: 

Sinusoidal waves: 
\begin{align*}
    f(z,t) = A\cos\br[2]{k(z-vt)+δ}, \eq{9.7}
\end{align*}
where $A$ is the aplitude, the cosine is the phase and $δ$ is the phase constant. 
$k$ is the wave number related to the wavelength $λ$:
\begin{align*}
    λ = \frac{2π}{k}. \eq{9.8}
\end{align*}
We can claculate the period $T$, the frequency $ν$ and the angular frequency $ω$: 
\begin{align*}
    T &= \frac{2\pi}{kv}, \eq{9.9} \\
    ν &= \frac{1}{T} = \frac{kv}{2\pi}, \eq{9.10} \\
    ω &= 2\pi ν = kv. \eq{9.11}
\end{align*}
The wavefunction can now be written as: 
\begin{align*}
    f(z,t) = A\cos(kz-ωt+δ). \eq{9.12}
\end{align*}

Complex notation: 
\begin{align*}
    f(z,t) = Re\br[2]{A\e{i(kz-ωt+δ)}} = Re\br[2]{\tilde{f}(z,t)}. \eq{9.16}
\end{align*}

Linear combination: 
\begin{align*}
    \tilde{f}(z,t) = \int_{\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{A}_k\e{i(kz-ωt+δ)}dk. \eq{9.20}
\end{align*}

If a wave hits a knot, that is where the tension on one side is deifferent from the other, 
the wave equation becomes: 
\begin{align*}
    \tilde{f}(z,t) = 
    \begin{cases*}
        \tilde{A}_I\e{i(k_1z-ωt)} + \tilde{A}_R\e{i(-k_1z-ωt)} & \text{for } z<0, \\
        \tilde{A}_T\e{i(k_2z-ωt)} & \text{for } z>0. 
    \end{cases*} \eq{9.25}
\end{align*}

\bil{fig9.5}

We can find the amplitudes of the reflected and transmitted, from the incomming wave: 
\begin{align*}
    \tilde{A}_R = \frac{v_2-v_1}{v_2+v_1}\tilde{A}_I, \quad \tilde{A}_T = \frac{2v_1}{v_2+v_1}\tilde{A}_I. \eq{9.30}
\end{align*}

The wave can be ploarized in the xy-plane with the vector: 
\begin{align*}
    \hat{\v{n}} = \cosθ\hat{\v{x}} + \sinθ\hat{\v{y}}. \eq{9.38}
\end{align*}

\section{Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum}
From Maxwell's equations (\ref{eq:7.52}, in vacuum) we can find that:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla^2 \v{E} = μ_0\ep \frac{\dd^2 \v{E}}{\dd t^2}, \quad \nabla^2\v{B} = μ_0\ep \frac{\dd^2 \v{B}}{\dd t^2}, \eq{9.41} 
\end{align*}
since $\nabla\cdot\v{E} = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\v{B} = 0$. 
Since these are wave equations, we find that: 
\begin{align*}
    v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{μ_0\ep}} = c. \eq{9.43}
\end{align*}

Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves, the magnetic and electric parts are perpendicular, 
therefore we get the relation: 
\begin{align*}
    B_0 = \frac{k}{ω}E_0=\frac{1}{c}E_0 \eq{9.48}
\end{align*}

We can generalize the equations for $B$ and $E$: 
\begin{align*}
    \begin{cases}
        \tilde{\v{E}}(\v{r},t) = \tilde{E}_0\e{i(\v{k}\cdot\v{r}-ωt)}\hat{\v{n}}, \\
        \tilde{\v{B}}(\v{r},t) = \tilde{B}_0\e{i(\v{k}\cdot\v{r}-ωt)}(\hat{\v{k}}\times \hat{\v{n}}),
    \end{cases} \eq{9.50}
\end{align*}
where $\hat{\v{k}}$ is the wave propegations vector, and $\hat{\v{n}}$ is the polaization vector. 

In a monocromatic wave the energy per unit volume is: 
\begin{align*}
    u = \frac{1}{2}\ep E^2 + \frac{1}{2μ_0}B^2 = \ep E_0^2, \eq{9.56}
\end{align*}
and the pointing vector is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{S} = \frac{1}{μ_0}(\v{E}\times\v{B}) = cu\hat{\v{k}}. \eq{9.58}
\end{align*}
Electromagnetic waves also carry momentum, and the momentum density is: 
\begin{align*}
    \v{g} &= \frac{1}{c^2}\v{S}, \eq{9.59} \\
    &= \frac{1}{c} u\hat{\v{k}}. \eq{9.60}
\end{align*}

The average of a cosine is $1/2$, so we get: 
\begin{align*}
    \br[3]{u} &= \frac{1}{2} \ep E_0^2, \eq{9.61} \\
    \br[3]{\v{S}} &= \frac{1}{2} \ep c E_0^2 \hat{\v{k}}, \eq{9.62} \\
    \br[3]{\v{g}} &= \frac{1}{2} \ep E_0^2 \hat{\v{k}}. \eq{9.63}
\end{align*}

The average power per unit area transported by the wave is called intensity: 
\begin{align*}
    I = \br[3]{\v{S}} = \frac{1}{2} \ep c E_0^2, \eq{9.64}
\end{align*}
and the average force per unit area (radiation pressure) is: 
\begin{align*}
    P = \frac{1}{A} \frac{Δp}{Δt} = \frac{1}{2} \ep E_0^2 = \frac{I}{c}. \eq{9.65}
\end{align*}
