\textbf{\Large{Experimental Physics II, Experiment 1: The Fresnel Relations}}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{adjustwidth}{35pt}{35pt}
    \indent The Fresnel Relations describe the reflection and transmission of light at the interface between two different media. By conducting an experiment,  the reflection and transmission coefficients for light incident on a dielectric-air and air-dielectric interface was measured. The dependence of the coefficients on the refractive indices of the media was analyzed, and the experimental results were compared with theoretical predictions to assess their accuracy. Overall, the findings support the validity of the Fresnel equations in describing light behavior at boundaries with minor deviation. 
    The polarization angle, also known as Brewster's angle from glass to air was found to be $35.103^\circ \pm 0.239^\circ$, and from air to glass: $56.546^\circ \pm 4.038^\circ$. The critical angle for glass into air, often referred to as the angle of total internal reflection, was found to be $44.663^\circ \pm 0.310^\circ$.
\end{adjustwidth}

\chapter{Introduction}
This report covers the Fresnel relations which describe the reflection and transmission of light at the interface between two media. In this experiment, we use a Red Diode Laser, directing it through a setup where the light is polarized before passing through a semicircular dielectric. We then measure the intensity and angle of the reflected and transmitted light for each polarization. These experimental measurements are compared to theoretical predictions based on the Fresnel relations, from which the theoretical intensities are calculated.

The measured angles in this experiment are denoted as \(\phi\), which correspond to the angular readings from the measurement device. However, for analysis using Fresnel’s equations, these must be converted into the true angles of incidence and refraction, \(\theta\), by considering geometric corrections based on the experimental setup, as seen in Figure \hyperref[fig:ia]{1}. Additionally, Snell's Law is used to relate the angles of incidence and refraction, given by \(n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2\), where \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are the refractive indices of the two media.

\subsection{Polarization and Light Interaction at a medium interface}
Light is an electromagnetic wave consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields that are perpendicular to both each other and the direction of propagation. In most natural sources, such as sunlight or incandescent light bulbs, light is unpolarized, meaning its electric field vectors oscillate in multiple, random planes. However, under certain conditions - such as when passing through a polarizing lens, as in this experiment - light can become polarized, meaning its electric field oscillates preferentially in a single direction.

Importantly, the polarization state of light influences the way it interacts at the interface. Light can be decomposed into two perpendicular components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{p-polarized light} (parallel polarization), where the electric field lies in the plane of incidence.
    \item \textbf{s-polarized light} (perpendicular polarization), where the electric field is perpendicular to the plane of incidence.
\end{itemize}

When light encounters a boundary between two different media, it undergoes reflection and refraction, ruled by the Fresnel relations. These equations describe how the intensities of reflected and transmitted light depend on the angle of incidence and the refractive indices of the media. 
The relationship between the angles of incidence $\theta_1$
and refraction $\theta_2$ is given by Snell’s Law\footnote{Unauthored, page 3.}:
\begin{align*}
    n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2. 
\end{align*}
Where $n_1$ and $n_2$ are the indices of refraction of the first and second media. For example, the refractive indices for air and the used dielectric are $n_{\text{air}} = 1.0$ and $n_{\text{glass}} = 1.5$. The angles $θ_1$ and $θ_2$, are the angles of incidence and refraction, which can be determined from the measured angles of the incident light as seen in (Figure \hyperref[fig:ia]{1}), where the measured angles must be added and subtracted for glass to air and air to glass interfaces respectively.
We denote the reflected light beam as $R$, with subscribts $p$ and $s$ to distinguish between p- and s-polarized light. Similarly, the transmitted beam is denoted by $T$.
\begin{align*}
    R_p &= \frac{\tan^2 (\theta_1 - \theta_2)}{\tan^2 (\theta_1 + \theta_2)},  \eq{7} \\
    T_p &= \frac{\sin 2\theta_1 \sin 2\theta_2}{\sin^2 (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \cos^2 (\theta_1 - \theta_2)},  \eq{8} \\
    R_s &= \frac{\sin^2 (\theta_1 - \theta_2)}{\sin^2 (\theta_1 + \theta_2)},  \eq{9} \\
    T_s &= \frac{\sin 2\theta_1 \sin 2\theta_2}{\sin^2 (\theta_1 + \theta_2)},  \eq{10}
\end{align*}

The Fresnel equations\footnote{Unauthored, page 4.} predict that these two components experience different reflection and transmission coefficients. This distinction leads to important polarization-dependent effects, including Brewster’s angle and the critical angle of total internal reflection.

\subsubsection{Brewster's Angle and the Critical Angle}
An interesting phenomenon occurs when the reflected light is fully polarized. This happens at a special angle known as Brewster’s angle (or the polarization angle). At this angle, the reflected wave consists entirely of s-polarized light because the p-polarized component is completely transmitted into the second medium. The condition for Brewster’s angle is derived from the Fresnel equations and occurs when the reflected and refracted beams are perpendicular to each other. Mathematically, Brewster’s angle is given by:
\begin{align*}
    \theta_B = \arctan\br{\frac{n_2}{n_1}}. \eq{12}
\end{align*}

A related and equally important phenomenon occurs when light moves from a medium of higher refractive index to one with a lower refractive index, which can result in total internal reflection. This phenomenon happens when the angle of incidence exceeds a critical value, known as the critical angle $\theta_C$. At this angle, the refracted beam travels along the boundary, and any further increase in the angle of incidence results in complete reflection of the light within the first medium. The critical angle $\theta_C$ is determined by Snell’s law and is given by:
\begin{align*}
    \theta_C = \arcsin\br{\frac{1}{n}}, \eq{13}
\end{align*}
Where $n$ is the refractive index of the medium in which the light is initially traveling, typically the media with the higher refractive index. If the angle of incidence exceeds $\theta_C$, no refraction occurs, and all light is reflected back into the original medium as can be seen in Figure \hyperref[fig:CrAn]{2}.

\subsection{General setup and safety}
The experiment is conducted using a series of optical components arranged in a specific order as described in the list of \hyperref[sec:equ]{Equipment}, and shown in Figure \hyperref[fig:setup]{3}. Figure 3 does not, however, show the electrical connections, as the detector is linked to the picoscope. We found, that the intensities recorded by picoscope became more stable when minimizing outside light interference. For this reason, we conducted the measurements with the lights off and the blinds closed.

The setup process begins with aligning the angular scales. The large scale is set to zero, and the small turntable is adjusted for proper alignment making the dielectric stand normal to the angle of incidence. The polarizer is set to the appropriate polarization of equal p- and s-polarization -  a method that will be detailed in \hyperref[sec:cal]{Calibration}. The laser is then turned on, and the beam is directed at the detector, with adjustments made to the beam’s focus and direction to ensure accurate measurements.

\subsubsection{Conducting measurements}
To measure the reflection and transmission coefficients ($R_p,\text{ } T_p, \text{ }R_s \text{ and } T_s$), we adjusted the incident angle $\theta_1$ by rotating the small turntable holding the dielectric. The detector was moved until the reflected or transmitted beam was captured. For each angle, we recorded the intensity of the reflected and transmitted light, ensuring maximum signal by fine-tuning the detector’s position and height. In some instances this was troublesome, as we experienced some bleeding of the beam, and it was therefore unable to fit entirely in the detector. The intensity data was captured through picoscope, which could have allowed us to obtain an average intensity value at each position, and given the standard deviation of each measurement. To compare our data with the theoretical values from the Fresnel relations, we adjusted some angle measurements to ensure accurate calculation of the reflection and transmission coefficients. The angular scale readings required this correction, as certain values were misinterpreted - for example, a reading of 167° actually corresponded to 13°, which needed to be accounted for during the analysis.

\subsubsection{Class II Laser Safety}
As the experiment used a Class II laser (visible light, $<$1mW), precautions were taken to prevent eye injury. While these lasers are safe under normal conditions, prolonged direct exposure can be harmful. The following safety measures\footnote{Drewsen: Laser safety measures from introductory lecture to the course Experimental Physics II.} were followed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Horizontal alignment away from eye level to prevent unintended exposure.
    \item Removal of reflective items such as jewelry, and enhanced caution when introducing items into beam path.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration} \label{sec:cal}
The calibration of the experimental setup is crucial for ensuring accurate measurements of s- and p-polarized light. Since our laser source primarily emits p-polarized light, an adjustment is necessary to achieve an equal intensity distribution between the two polarizations.

The calibration process involves adjusting several components of the setup, which can be challenging at first. Because the detector saturates at 5V, it's important to either reduce the amount of light passing through the collimating slits (by selecting a smaller slit size) or decrease the laser intensity. Next, by measuring the unpolarized intensity (which can exceed the saturation value, as all unwanted polarizations are later blocked), the appropriate setting for the polarizing lens can be determined. This is done by alternating between p- and s-polarization using the TAPL, adjusting the Polarizing Lens until the intensities for both polarizations are roughly equal - ensuring they each stay below the 5V saturation threshold. In our case, we found equal intensities at around 4V for each polarization, which was sufficient for good data without saturating the detector.

Using the experimentally obtained angles and intensity measurements, we performed a nonlinear least-squares fit to Fresnel’s relations with the refractive index of glass, $n_{\text{glass}}$, as the sole fitting parameter. The fitting was conducted using Python’s \texttt{curve\_fit} function, which minimizes the sum of squared residuals between the experimental data and the theoretical model. To assess the quality of the fit, we computed the reduced chi-squared ($\chi^2_{red}$), which indicates a great fit for values close to 1 - which we did obtain for the s-polarizations of the air to glass interface with values (1.12, 1.08), the p-polarizations however were not as good, yielding values (6.11, 3.47). This could be due to the fact that we used the fit residuals to compute the standard deviation, and not deviations from the data.

Figure \hyperref[fig:ag]{4} and Figure \hyperref[fig:ga]{6} display the fitted curves alongside the measured data and theoretical predictions for both air-to-glass and glass-to-air interfaces. The reflectance measurements exhibit strong agreement with theory, as reflected in the obtained values of $n_{\text{glass}}$ (see Figure \hyperref[tab:fit]{5}).  

However, the transmission data deviates more significantly from the theoretical expectations. Despite this, the fit remains statistically good, which can be attributed to the constraints imposed by the Fresnel equations. The fitting function inherently obeys the boundary conditions at the interface, which might lead to an overestimation of the fit accuracy, despite inconsistencies in the data.  

For the air-to-glass transition, the fitted refractive index is close to the expected theoretical value of $n_{\text{glass}}$. In contrast, for the glass-to-air transition, the fitted refractive index is slightly lower than expected, despite a small statistical uncertainty in the fit. This suggests that while the fit conforms well mathematically, potential systematic errors or misalignment in the experimental setup may have influenced the transmission measurements. Additionally, we calculated the refractive index using Snell’s law for each interface, yielding $n_{\text{glass}} = 1.295$ for air-to-glass and $n_{\text{glass}} = 1.468$ for glass-to-air. The air-to-glass result deviates significantly from both the theoretical and fitted values, despite a good-looking dataset. This inconsistency suggests that additional experimental uncertainties, such as alignment errors, may have impacted the transmission measurements.

\subsection{Uncertainties}
The transmission data exhibited significant variation in intensity, especially for the second experiment (glass to air interface). For the first experiment (air to glass), the variation was somewhat systematic, while in the second experiment, the data showed random variation with some points deviating significantly. During the experiment, we noticed that the reflected light was not centered on the detector, which we corrected for in the reflectance measurements. However, this adjustment was not applied to the transmission part, potentially leading to missed laser intensity at certain angles and resulting in lower-than-expected transmission values.

\chapter{Discussion}\label{sec:dis}
The experimental results generally align with the theoretical Fresnel equations, particularly in the reflectance data, where the measured values closely follow theoretical predictions. However, some deviations are observed in the transmission measurements, likely due to external light interference or potential misalignment of the detector. Additionally, the inability to obtain averaged intensity values directly from the Picoscope introduced uncertainty, as readings had to be manually recorded from a fluctuating signal.

We also observed that the fitted refractive index was lower than expected in the second experiment, which suggests possible issues with the dielectric material. Since a different sample was used compared to the first experiment, as we carried them out in different lab sessions, inconsistencies in material properties such as scratches could have contributed to this inconsistency. Despite these variations, the reflectance data, particularly from the first experiment, provides a more stable estimate of the refractive index, reinforcing the validity of the Fresnel relations.

\subsubsection{Potential improvements}
\begin{itemize} \item Improving the alignment of both the incident beam and the detector to reduce systematic errors. \item Conducting measurements in a controlled dark environment to minimize external light contamination. \item Extending measurement durations to reduce statistical uncertainties, using  Picoscope's function to calculate the standard deviation of intensity values for a given incident angle dataset. \item Ensuring the dielectric surface is free of scratches and irregularities, as these could affect the measured intensities. \item Optimizing the use of the Picoscope to obtain averaged intensity values, thereby minimizing uncertainties from fluctuating signals. \end{itemize}

\chapter{Conclusion}
The experiment confirmed the Fresnel relations by measuring reflection and transmission coefficients at a dielectric interface. The refractive index of glass was, on average, determined as $n = 1.514 \pm 0.538$ from experiment 1, and $n = 1.423 \pm 0.007$ from experiment 2, showing minor deviations from the expected value of $n = 1.5$. Brewster and critical angles were measured as $35.103 \pm 0.239^{\circ}$ and $44.663 \pm 0.310^{\circ}$, for glass into air, aligning well with theory of $33.69^{\circ}$ and $41.81^{\circ}$. Future improvements should focus on detector alignment and reducing ambient light interference.
